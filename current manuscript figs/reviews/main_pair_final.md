# Editorial review of the two main-text candidates

Reviewed 17 October 2026 against the two actual PNGs, their current candidate
documents, repository/manuscript guidance, comparison status and migration-figure
context. The completed Holmes mesh and imbalance summaries were read for their
editorial implications; this review does not independently audit those results.
No simulations or candidate-document edits were performed.

**Both are acceptable candidates for discussion. They answer different questions,
and neither completes the explanation of WGD-associated migration persistence.**
Candidate 1 has the stronger connection to enlargement. Candidate 2 is the
clearer figure for the user's proposed intervention on challenger position and
the sharper demonstration that activation and subsequent spatial competition
are different events. Which belongs in the main text depends on which of those
claims we decide the text should make.

| Intended claim | Stronger candidate | What the reader actually learns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enlargement changes the incumbent's chemical organization and response to a shared pool | [Candidate 1](../main_candidate_1_20261017.md) | A taller Goryachev incumbent gains local substrate-conversion sensitivity; a broader Wave-pinning incumbent changes much less in that local property. |
| Challenger separation changes the later interaction between already activated sites | [Candidate 2](../main_candidate_2_20261017.md) | Comparable early activation can be followed by merger or by two retained sites under the same cue and starting state. |
| Enlargement preserves one migration direction for longer | Neither | Neither figure measures directional persistence or supplies the mapping from its spatial chemical outcome to migration. |

## Candidate 1: a substantive mechanism with a deliberately narrow experiment

The useful result is more precise than “a larger pole contains more material.”
Enlargement produces different architectures: Goryachev becomes much taller,
whereas Wave-pinning mainly becomes broader. The native conversion laws then give
those architectures different local responses to available inactive material.
The current factorization makes this concrete: Goryachev's surplus-weighted
conversion coefficient increases 16.81-fold while surplus per core damage falls
slightly. The large normalized rebound is therefore not explained by simply
having more surplus per unit damage. The lower opposite-end inactive
concentration also reduces the initial added source from an equal local cue.
Those are intelligible, model-specific consequences of enlargement.

The revised negative active-field contribution is scientifically valuable. It
prevents the figure from quietly equating a taller incumbent with stronger
intrinsic restoration. Here the positive chemical rebound is supplied by
conversion of the inactive surplus left after the specified disturbance and
return. “Substrate capture” must retain that defined meaning; it is not an
additional binding mechanism.

The weak bridge is still the choice of functional challenge. A brief, global
inactive-profile removal and exact return creates very small, unequal active
damage. Dividing the immediate rate by that damage answers a local response
question. It does not show damage tolerance, a comparable whole-recovery time
between models, or victory over a competing site. The figure can motivate a
competitive advantage, but cannot label that advantage as an observed outcome.
Likewise, the opposite-end cue identity establishes the initial source, not the
challenger's eventual ability to establish itself.

Visually, the profiles and actual response traces earn their space. The final
row is more demanding: near-coincident net and surplus curves, nearly invisible
small terms, two model-specific scales and algebraic allocation language place
substantial interpretive weight on the caption. The coefficient factorization
now in the document should be the direct verbal takeaway. The reader should not
have to infer the interesting property from the bookkeeping. No extra panel or
experiment is needed to make that editorial choice.

## Candidate 2: the stronger spatial argument, with no automatic persistence sign

This figure makes a consequential distinction visible without requiring a
specialist to decode a rate allocation. Both challenger locations activate;
the nearer pair subsequently fills the intervening valley into one broad cap,
whereas the farther pair retains two narrower caps. The incumbent is shared
exactly, and the full fields prevent “activity at the old position” from being
mistaken for survival of an unchanged incumbent. Similar final peak heights with
narrower caps show reorganization of activity rather than two copies of the
original cap.

That is a stronger response to the user's distance proposal than a duration
curve labelled “farther takes longer.” It identifies a change in the kind of
response. The completed [mesh](../../polarity1d/manuscript/figures/length_mechanisms_20261008/dev/holmes_distance_robustness_20261017/supporting/metadata/MESH_RESULTS.md)
and [conserved-imbalance](../../polarity1d/manuscript/figures/length_mechanisms_20261008/dev/holmes_distance_robustness_20261017/supporting/metadata/IMBALANCE_RESULTS.md)
checks strengthen this as a resolved finite-window observation. They support
retaining the candidate; an asymptotic stability theorem is not required for
that claim. The candidate now reports the completed targeted checks, including
the transient mesh discrepancy and matched redistribution continuations; the
earlier pending-check presentation issue is resolved.

The conceptual limit is also sharper. A second active site can coexist with the
old one while substantially changing whole-cell polarity. Retaining the old site
does not mean retaining the original migration direction. Indeed, the two-cap
outcome makes a single-front interpretation harder. A main-text argument for
persistence must address that mapping rather than treating coexistence as
protection by default.

“Distance” here is centre separation on a ring, with both complementary routes
changing together. The basal incumbent is broad, and moving the cue also changes
its relation to the incumbent's tails and interfaces. The intervention is clean
as a change of cue location; it does not isolate molecular travel time or explain
the chemical reason for valley filling. The current figure establishes the
spatial result without completing that further mechanistic account. It also
does not show that enlargement moves a biological cell between these two regimes.

The four-panel layout is readable. Its main presentation hazard is that the
heatmaps end 1000 s after withdrawal while the profiles include the much later
endpoint. The caption must preserve that distinction, as it currently does.
The long observation and perturbation support belong in linked material, not
additional rows that obscure the early interaction.

## Anticipated user critique — explicitly hypothetical

These are possible objections in the user's direct voice, not actual quotations.

- **Candidate 1:** “You have shown me why a tall autocatalytic peak converts more
  substrate. What have you shown about the fate of an actual challenger?” The
  present answer is initial remote availability and conditional incumbent
  rebound. Successful suppression remains an inference.
- **Candidate 1:** “Of course the system recovers when you put the material back.
  Why should I care?” The informative part is the strongly length-dependent
  conversion response, despite slightly less surplus per damage, and its
  contrast with broadening in Wave-pinning. Recovery's sign alone is not the
  interesting finding.
- **Candidate 2:** “Closer active regions merging sounds obvious. What have I
  learned?” Both regions activate; the later spatial outcome changes while the
  starting cell, pools and cue remain fixed. That is a concrete result about
  interaction modes. It should not be advertised as a complete explanation of
  their chemical cause.
- **Candidate 2:** “Why is having two fronts evidence for a more persistent
  migration direction?” It is not yet such evidence. The figure demonstrates
  retained local activity and changed spatial organization; its migration
  consequence remains open.

**Editorial recommendation:** present both concrete alternatives to the user.
Choose candidate 1 if the desired main-text claim is what enlargement changes
chemically; choose candidate 2 if it is why activation and later site interaction
must be distinguished. Do not join them into an implied causal sequence that
has not been shown. A figure can be worth keeping before it proves the full
biological explanation; the outstanding decision is which limited claim is
important enough for this manuscript's main text.
