# WGD manuscript: narrative and claims supported by the round-3 figure set

Oct 23, 2026 · @Richard

Across five polarity models with unchanged local kinetics, directionally polarized cells resist reorientation upon challenge by opposing cues and, under fluctuating input, maintain their heading for longer. Model analyses predict that resistance to reorientation arises through one of three routes: slower initiation of a competing domain, saturation that forces a challenger to expand, or a slow mechanical memory. They predict that persistence under fluctuating input arises because polarity domains drift no faster, and in some models more slowly, around a larger perimeter. The analyses also suggest experiments to discriminate these possibilities. Regardless of the mechanism, the enhanced directional persistence predicted by all models is consistent with published migration data. Follow-up experiments in a WGD+/− cell pair are planned.

This maps the claims the round-3 figure set (outline\_v9, commit `87cb5f5` on `codex/round3-outline-compact-20261023`) supports as it stands. ## 1. Larger cells need longer cues to reorient (Fig. 1)

- We polarized model cells of different lengths, with local kinetics unchanged (Fig. 1A).
- Each cell was then challenged with a finite opposing cue of one of three shapes: fixed width, width proportional to length, or a constant-slope ramp (Fig. 1B).
- For each length, we determined the shortest pulse that produced lasting reorientation **(not shown; duration curves in Fig. S1)**.
- Under fixed-width and proportional-width cues, the required pulse duration increased with length in all five models (Fig. 1C). The rank correlation was perfect in four models and weaker in deBelly (+0.29 to +0.55), whose duration curves are non-monotone (Fig. S1).
- Under the ramp, the length dependence was model-specific (Fig. 1C). Because the ramp peak increases with length (Fig. 1B, right), this comparison confounds size with cue dose.
- The magnitude of the increase in required duration **(not shown)**.

## 2. Larger cells resist replacement by three routes, set by how each model stores the extra active material (Fig. 2)

- Larger cells contain more active material, which the models accommodate in one of two ways (Fig. 2A). Wave-pinning, Holmes, deBelly and Spring broaden the active domain at an approximately fixed peak; Goryachev maintains domain width and increases peak height. These two scaling modes impose different requirements on a challenging domain.
- **Missing step: the mechanistic basis of the two scaling modes (not shown; candidate panel: Wave-pinning vs Goryachev net-activation maps, length-mechanisms report Fig. 3).**
  - In Goryachev, the taller incumbent depletes the inactive pool: opposite-end inactive concentration falls three-fold between L9 and L39. An equal cue therefore activates one third as much material, and the local activation threshold for a challenger rises from 18 to 58.
  - In Wave-pinning, active concentration saturates at a fixed plateau and net activation is confined to the domain boundary, at a level independent of length. A challenger therefore cannot prevail by increasing its height and must instead expand.
- Under continuous opposing cues, replacement time increased 5–12-fold from L19 to L39 in every model, at every cue width for which both lengths were measured (Fig. 2C).
- The Goryachev constraint appears as delayed initiation, approximately 2.5-fold slower at L39 (Fig. 2B). Initiation time was independent of length in Wave-pinning, Holmes and Spring, and apparently in deBelly.
- The expansion constraint appears as a cue-width penalty. At equal cue area, narrow cues were 3.8-fold (Wave-pinning) and 45-fold (Holmes) slower than broad cues at L19, and failed within the simulation horizon at larger lengths. In Goryachev, deBelly and Spring, replacement time varied by at most 1.6-fold across widths (Fig. 2C).
- Pulse trains distinguish three response classes (Fig. 2D, E):
  - **Goryachev:** challenger activity collapses during each gap and must be re-initiated by each pulse.
  - **Wave-pinning and Holmes:** challenger activity is retained across gaps.
  - **deBelly and Spring:** visible challenger activity is lost during gaps, yet subsequent pulses still produce gains and the challenger ultimately takes over.
- Outside Goryachev, the step that slows with size is removal of the incumbent, not initiation of the challenger. Initiation was length-independent (Fig. 2B) while replacement slowed 5–12-fold (Fig. 2C), and challengers in Wave-pinning and Holmes retained their gains across gaps (Fig. 2D, E). This asymmetry, in which challengers start readily but incumbents resist removal, recurs under fluctuating input (Section 4).
- **Missing step: the state that deBelly retains across gaps (not shown; candidate panel: deBelly spring/dashpot graphic, length-mechanisms report Fig. 10).** Membrane strain and cortical flow persist after Rac activity decays, indicating a mechanical rather than chemical memory. Spring, also a mechanical model, presumably behaves similarly.
- These signatures predict distinct biosensor readouts in WGD− and WGD+ cells (Fig. 2F; prospective). Because Fig. 2E shows L39 only, the size-dependent differences depicted in F are **(not shown)**.

## 3. Larger rings turn more slowly toward a new cue; in some models their boundary also responds less to weak input, and this sets their drift (Fig. 3)

This section describes the round-5 Figure 3 (commit `769eac59`). ***Bold italic marks the narrative expected after the next revision.***

- On rings of fixed circumference, a continuous cue was placed at varying distances from the edge of the incumbent domain, and we measured the time required to rotate halfway toward the cue (Fig. 3A, B).
- Two turning regimes emerged depending on whether the cue overlapped the incumbent domain (Fig. 3A). An overlapping cue displaced the incumbent toward it, whereas a separated cue nucleated a second domain that subsequently replaced the incumbent. After cue withdrawal, both relaxed to a single domain at the new heading (Fig. 3A, bottom row).
- Larger rings reoriented more slowly at every cue position in all four models (Fig. 3B). Per volume doubling, half-turn time increased approximately 1.5-fold in Goryachev, 2–3-fold in Wave-pinning and Spring, and 3–7-fold in Holmes **(fold change not plotted)**.
- Once the cue was separated from the incumbent, further separation had little effect in Wave-pinning, Holmes and Spring: half-turn time varied by less than about 10% across gaps. Distance therefore acted mainly by determining the turning regime. Goryachev was the exception, with half-turn time increasing 2–4-fold with gap. Spontaneous secondary domains under fluctuating input behaved similarly: their probability of takeover did not depend on distance from the incumbent edge (Fig. 4E ii).
- A weak, brief input at the domain boundary displaced the entire domain permanently (Fig. 3C). The opposite boundary moved by the same amount, and displacement was proportional to pulse amplitude between 0.5- and 2-fold the nominal peak (supplement). The linear range beyond this was not tested. ***A sweep of pulse amplitude and duration establishes the input strength at which this linear response fails, and whether that limit depends on size.***
- ***The displacement produced by the same weak pulse at every position around the ring defines a susceptibility map (revised Fig. 3C). Edge pulses fall on this map, and pulses placed within the plateau and in the basal region test it away from the boundary.***
- Boundary susceptibility declined with size to a model-specific extent. Terminal displacement per unit dose at the largest size, relative to the smallest, was 0.70 in Wave-pinning, 0.91 in Holmes, 0.99 in Spring and 0.09 in Goryachev (Fig. 3C; insets show the integrated response).
- A linear-response calculation predicted the drift of the dominant domain under fluctuating input without fitting (Fig. 3D, top). Noise was treated as a superposition of weak inputs at every position, each displacing the domain according to the linearized native model, combined with the covariance of the Figure 4 input. Predicted drift diffusivity relative to that measured in Fig. 4 had medians of 1.02 (Wave-pinning), 1.08 (Holmes) and 1.07 (Spring). Goryachev predictions deviated by up to two orders of magnitude.
- Agreement in absolute diffusivity spans several decades mainly because drift scales with the square of input strength. The size-normalized comparison is therefore the stringent test (Fig. 3D, bottom). ***The revised figure shows the susceptibility map and which input positions contribute to drift, establishing whether input within the plateau or in the basal region contributes appreciably.***
- The two boundaries moved together under fluctuating input (increment correlation 0.98–1.00 in two exemplar seeds; supplement). A prediction that treated them as independent underestimated drift approximately two-fold (Fig. 3D, open symbols).
- The size dependence of drift followed from boundary susceptibility (Fig. 3D, bottom). In Wave-pinning, the predicted C60/C20 drift ratio was 0.48–0.49 at every input with f ≤ 0.01, against 0.45–0.53 measured. This equals the square of the boundary-susceptibility fold (0.70² = 0.49), indicating that the shape of the response is size-independent and only its magnitude falls. The predicted ratio was 0.83 in Holmes (measured 0.77–0.96) and 1.00 in Spring (measured 1.03–1.13 at f ≤ 0.005, 1.39 at f = 0.01). Goryachev ratios were not predicted.
- In Wave-pinning, the predicted drift ratio implies a size exponent of approximately 2.6 across C20–C60, matching the 2.5–3 measured in Fig. 4B **(computed; not plotted)**. The persistence of larger Wave-pinning rings in excess of the geometric expectation is therefore accounted for by reduced susceptibility of the domain boundary. ***A panel comparing predicted and measured local exponents shows this for each model.***
- Fig. 3B and Fig. 3C–D use different size grids, so a matched-size relation between strong-cue turning and weak-input susceptibility is not established. ***At three matched sizes, the size fold in half-turn time is compared with the size fold in susceptibility.***
- The two turning regimes have counterparts under fluctuating input (Section 4). At weak input, heading was lost almost entirely through motion of the dominant domain; at stronger input, increasingly through the appearance and loss of domains (Fig. 4E iii).
- These analyses suggest two cellular assays. First, photoactivation at offsets from straddling to separated relative to the leading-edge boundary, with half-turn time as the readout (Fig. 3A, B). Second, a weak, brief photoactivation pulse at the boundary, tracking both boundaries through withdrawal. The squared ratio of boundary responses between WGD+ and WGD− cells would predict the ratio of leading-edge drift **(assumes size-independent input statistics)**.
- Resolution of coexisting domains after cue withdrawal **(not shown)**.

## 4. Larger cells maintain heading longer under fluctuating input, an advantage that diminishes as input strengthens (Fig. 4)

This section describes the round-5 Figure 4 (commit `769eac59`). ***Bold italic marks the narrative expected after the next revision.***

- We summarized the size dependence of persistence by an exponent α: the rate at which heading decorrelates, k, scales with circumference C as k ∝ C^−α, so a larger α means a stronger advantage for larger cells. α is estimated locally from three adjacent circumferences, so each size has its own estimate; where a single power law holds, these coincide. Simple null models of polarity-domain wandering predict distinct values of α (Fig. 4A):
  - Drift at a fixed physical diffusivity gives α = 2.
  - Relocation at a size-independent rate gives α = 0.
  - Relocation at a rate proportional to perimeter gives α = −1.
- Drift dominates at weak input, giving α ≈ 2 (the drift regime). If relocations increase more steeply with input, they dominate at strong input and drive α toward 0 or −1 **(null mixtures not shown)**.
- We define noise tolerance as the input at which heading correlation over a fixed reference lag falls to 0.5. Under drift alone it increases in proportion to circumference, three-fold from C20 to C60.
- Wave-pinning, Holmes and Spring show the drift regime at weak input (Fig. 4B). α was approximately 2 in Holmes and Spring, and 2.5–3 in Wave-pinning, where larger cells therefore gain more persistence than drift geometry alone predicts. As input strengthened, α declined toward 1 in all three, although it did not reach 0 within the tested range. Goryachev yielded few resolved estimates, most of them flagged as forced.
- Larger rings tolerated stronger input (Fig. 4B, right). From C20 to C60, noise tolerance rose 2.2-fold in Holmes and 2.4-fold in Wave-pinning, below the drift-only fold of 3; 3.1-fold in Spring, matching drift alone; and 4.1-fold in Goryachev, exceeding every null model.
- At inputs near the transition, the larger ring maintained its heading markedly longer in all four models (Fig. 4C).
- Drift of the dominant domain was measured from the mean squared displacement of its centroid while it remained dominant (Fig. 4D, top; normalized to the smallest ring at the same input). At weak input, size reduced drift about two-fold in Wave-pinning and far more strongly in Goryachev, but not in Holmes or Spring. This is consistent with the α values above, and Fig. 3D accounts for it: drift follows from the response of the domain boundary to weak input, which declines with size in Wave-pinning and Goryachev and hardly at all in Holmes and Spring (Section 3). Strong, sustained cues slow with size in all four models (Fig. 3B) because they act outside this linear range. At strong input, drift instead rose with size, up to about four-fold in Holmes and two-fold in Spring.
- Drift behaved as a small-signal response. For f ≤ 0.01, drift diffusivity scaled as f^1.96–1.99 in Wave-pinning, f^1.94–1.95 in Holmes and f^1.87–1.93 in Spring, as expected for a linear response to input whose variance scales with f² (Fig. 4D legend). Goryachev did not scale this way, consistent with a pinned spike.
- The strongly suppressed drift in larger Goryachev rings may reflect the same inactive-pool depletion that delays initiation (Fig. 2B) **(hypothesis)**.
- In the drift regime, heading loss is explained. Where f ≤ 0.01 and multiple domains occupied at most 5% of frames, drift plus counted replacements predicted the observed rate of heading decorrelation to within 15% in 45 of 47 conditions (Fig. 4D, bottom). Heading loss was allocated almost entirely to motion of the dominant domain (Fig. 4E iii; Wave-pinning C20 at f = 0.001: 0.999).
- Above a threshold input, secondary domains appeared (Fig. 4E i). Certified births per unit perimeter did not change consistently with size: they increased in Spring, decreased in Goryachev, and had overlapping bounds in Wave-pinning and Holmes. Multiple domains were nonetheless more frequent in larger rings: in Holmes at f = 0.03, the multiple-domain fraction rose from 7% at C20 to 69% at C60 **(computed from Fig. 4 data; not plotted)**.
- Newborn domains were less likely to take over in larger rings, regardless of their distance from the incumbent (Fig. 4E ii). Pooled over f > 0.01, the probability of takeover within 20 noise correlation times fell from 0.55 to 0.22 in Wave-pinning, 0.50 to 0.25 in Holmes, 0.75 to 0.42 in Spring and 0.39 to 0.07 in Goryachev. At a single transition input the decline was steeper: at f = 0.03, from 0.47 to 0.02 in Wave-pinning and from 0.51 to 0.14 in Holmes **(computed from Fig. 4 data; not plotted)**. This is the asymmetry of Fig. 2 under fluctuating input: challengers arise readily, but in larger rings the incumbent is harder to remove. ***The revised figure shows takeover probability at one transition input per model.***
- As input strengthened, heading loss shifted from domain motion to changes in domain membership and to a remainder not captured by the region description, and did so at lower input in larger rings (Fig. 4E iii). At f = 0.03, the motion share fell from 0.76 to 0.52 between the smallest and largest Wave-pinning rings and from 0.79 to 0.29 in Holmes, while the remainder rose to 0.13 and 0.37. Changes in the relative amounts of coexisting domains contributed at most 0.08. Shifts in balance between coexisting domains therefore do not account for the loss of heading.
- Whether drift and counted takeovers account for heading loss where multiple domains predominate remains undetermined. Unscored takeover angles leave the comparison bounded, with agreement within 15% possible in at most 24 of 33 such conditions (Fig. 4 legend). ***The revised figure extends the comparison to every input, with these bounds shown, alongside the remainder share at each size.***
- The growing remainder indicates that describing activity as discrete domains fails at strong input, particularly in larger rings. Either the reduction of each region to a centroid and an amount is too coarse, or activity ceases to be organized into discrete domains. ***Recomputing the remainder from exact region moments distinguishes the two.***
- ***A description that does not require domain detection explains the strong-input regime. Heading is the phase of the first Fourier mode of the activity profile, so its persistence is set by the decorrelation rate γ of that mode at wavenumber q = 2π/C, and α equals the local slope of ln γ against ln q. In the drift regime a single domain fills the ring, γ ∝ q² and α = 2. At strong input, activity forms local patches with a size-independent correlation length; below the corresponding wavenumber, modes decorrelate at a size-independent rate and α approaches 0. Larger rings read heading at smaller q and therefore leave the drift regime at lower input. Decorrelation rates from all sizes fall on a single curve against q, and its local slope reproduces the measured α. If this is not supported, the strong-input regime is reported as unexplained.***
- These results suggest observables for the migration experiments **(speculative)**:
  - diffusion of the lateral edges of the leading active or protrusive region, and their response to a weak boundary pulse (Section 3);
  - the takeover probability of secondary protrusions, by cell size;
  - ***the correlation length and decorrelation rate of protrusive or biosensor activity along the cell edge, from edge kymographs;***
  - heading autocorrelation.

  If secondary protrusions rarely take over and edge fluctuations are size-independent, the models predict persistence rising roughly with the square of size, about 1.6-fold per WGD. ***Where edge activity is patchy, that advantage falls toward zero, and WGD+ cells, with more perimeter per correlation length, enter that regime at lower stimulus strength.***

## 5. Published migration data fit greater persistence in enlarged cells (Fig. 5)

- Enlarged or polyploid groups were not consistently faster: speed was lower in Xuan and Mallin and higher in Jemaà. They did, however, show greater directness (Mallin 3.1-fold, Hosny 1.7-fold) or greater long-lag displacement (Xuan 2.1-fold) (Fig. 5A).
- In Xuan, the mean squared displacement of the enlarged group was initially lower but exceeded that of the reference group beyond approximately 2.3 h, the profile expected for slower but more persistent motion (Fig. 5B).
- Ornstein–Uhlenbeck fits inferred longer velocity memory in all five comparisons (pooled fold approximately 16; Fig. 5E), robust to the choice of prior and pooling (Fig. 5F).
- The fit does not reproduce the shape or crossover of the Xuan curves (Fig. 5B), so the inferred fold is conditional on the model.

## What the set doesn't yet support

- **No figure connects imposed replacement (Figs 1–3) to noise-driven persistence (Fig. 4).** The link must be made in the text or shown directly. The revised Fig. 4E strengthens one candidate: where multiple active domains predominate, heading is lost faster than drift and relocation of the dominant domain predict, which suggests that competition among coexisting domains, the process studied in Figs 2–3, drives the decline in α. The excess of secondary-domain births over takeovers in larger rings, which mirrors the readily started but rarely successful challengers of Fig. 2, would be the most direct bridge **(hypothesis; not shown)**.
- **Magnitude (not shown).** At α ≈ 2, a single WGD (a 1.26-fold increase in circumference) would reduce the rate of heading decorrelation only about 1.6-fold, whereas the published OU persistence fold is approximately 16.
- **The connection between simulated heading persistence and migration persistence remains a hypothesis.**
- **The figures do not support** claims that distance matters beyond determining the turning regime, that multiple domains cause the decline in α, or any specific mechanism of heading loss in the multiple-domain regime.

* **Figs 3–4 lack experimental signatures.** Unlike Fig. 2F, neither figure proposes a measurement that would discriminate the mechanisms it describes. The summary's claim that the analyses suggest discriminating experiments currently rests on Fig. 2 alone. Candidate signatures are proposed in Sections 3–4: the edge-pulse response, and the statistics of secondary-domain births and takeovers.

## The thread in one paragraph

Across five models, larger cells resist reorientation (Figs 1–3). Under fluctuating input they maintain their heading for longer, by approximately the amount expected from drift alone at weak input, and this advantage diminishes as input strengthens and multiple domains appear, through a process not captured by drift or counted relocations, plausibly shifting balance among secondary domains that, as under imposed cues, start readily but rarely displace the incumbent (Fig. 4). Published migration data show the increase in persistence this would predict (Fig. 5).
